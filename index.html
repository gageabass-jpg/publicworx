Commonwealth v. Lindsay Clancy

A sourced tracker of a live Massachusetts criminal trial.

Snapshot compiled Aug 25, 2026 · verify against live reporting before relying on any detail.

Active trial — no verdict. As of this snapshot the jury has not deliberated. Lindsay Clancy has pleaded not guilty and is legally presumed innocent unless and until a jury finds otherwise. Both sides agree she caused her children's deaths; the trial is a dispute over criminal responsibility, not over what happened. Language here is deliberately neutral (charged, alleged, testified) — not "murder" as a settled fact.

  4. Jury deliberations — a unanimous verdict is required
  5. Verdict returned — then, if applicable, sentencing or commitment
The question the jury must answer. Not whether Clancy killed her children — both sides agree she did — but whether she is criminally responsible. Under Massachusetts law the burden is on the prosecution to prove criminal responsibility beyond a reasonable doubt; if jurors have a reasonable doubt that she could appreciate the wrongfulness of her actions (or conform her conduct to the law), the verdict is not-guilty by reason of lack of criminal responsibility.

Possible outcomes

As outlined by legal analysts covering the case. The jury may reach a different verdict on each of the three counts.

Guilty — first-degree murder

If found criminally responsible & premeditated/deliberate

  • Carries a mandatory sentence of life in prison without the possibility of parole in Massachusetts.

Guilty — second-degree murder

Lesser included offense

  • Also a life sentence, but with eventual parole eligibility.

Not criminally responsible

The insanity ("NGRI") verdict the defense seeks

  • Not an acquittal to freedom. It typically leads to commitment to a secure state psychiatric facility, subject to periodic review hearings — potentially long-term.

Other possibilities

Depending on the judge's instructions

  • Jurors could convict on a lesser offense (e.g. manslaughter) on one or more counts, or fail to reach unanimity (a hung jury / mistrial) — which can lead to a retrial.

The two cases

The core dispute is not what happened but criminal responsibility — whether mental illness means Clancy should not be held criminally responsible under Massachusetts law.

Prosecution

Plymouth County District Attorney's Office

  • Argues the killings were planned and deliberate.
  • Points to evidence they say shows intent and awareness of wrongfulness.
  • Called a rebuttal psychiatrist disputing that Clancy met the standard for lacking criminal responsibility.
  • Seeks conviction on three counts of first-degree murder.

Defense

Atty. Kevin Reddington

  • Argues Clancy was not criminally responsible due to severe postpartum psychosis.
  • Testimony that she experienced delusions and heard a voice the night of the deaths.
  • Raises an overmedication theory — that her prescribed regimen worsened her state (see Disputed Claims).
  • Called mental-health professionals and treating clinicians.
What both sides accept: that Lindsay Clancy strangled her three children on Jan 24, 2023. The trial turns on her mental state and criminal responsibility, which is why psychiatric testimony has dominated the proceedings.

The husband: Patrick Clancy

Patrick Clancy — Lindsay's husband at the time, since divorced — is the case's pivotal lay witness. He made the 911 call, discovered the scene, and was the prosecution's first witness. He is a grieving parent and a witness; he faces no charges or allegations of wrongdoing.

How the prosecution used him

Direct examination

  • Established the tight ~54-minute timeline — the window in which the deaths occurred.
  • Testified Lindsay had told him she was suicidal and had intrusive thoughts about harming the children.
  • Described her worsening in Dec 2022 after a Seroquel increase, and a multi-day stay in a locked psychiatric ward in early January.

How the defense used him

Cross-examination (Atty. Reddington)

  • Drew out a portrait of a loving, devoted mother — including her social-media posts of the children.
  • Had him detail Lindsay's repeated efforts to get mental-health care in the months prior.
  • Used his account to support the argument that a well mother would not have harmed her children.
His public stance. In a 2024 New Yorker interview, Patrick Clancy said: "I wasn't married to a monster — I was married to someone who got sick," and publicly urged compassion. Prosecutors fought pretrial to obtain his statements to the magazine and the reporter's materials — one reason the trial was repeatedly delayed.

Key testimony: the expert psychiatrists

Because both sides agree Clancy caused the deaths, the verdict hinges on criminal responsibility — and that turned into a battle of forensic experts giving directly opposing readings of the same person. Their conclusions, as reported from the courtroom:

What they're actually arguing about (Massachusetts standard). A defendant lacks criminal responsibility if, because of a mental disease or defect, they lacked substantial capacity either to appreciate the wrongfulness of their conduct or to conform their conduct to the law. The experts split on both halves: was she psychotic, and — even if she was — could she still tell right from wrong?

Dr. Phillip Resnick

Defense · forensic psychiatrist, postpartum-disorders expert

  • Concluded Clancy was "frankly psychotic" the day of the deaths and labeled it postpartum psychosis.
  • Described a "command hallucination" — a voice directing her — and a "delusion of influence," a sense of an external force taking over her body.
  • Supports the defense's core claim: she could not appreciate the wrongfulness of her actions.

Paul Zeizel

Defense · clinical & forensic psychologist

  • Evaluated Clancy in early February 2023, shortly after the deaths.
  • Testified she was experiencing psychosis at the time and could not tell right from wrong.

Dr. Avram Mack

Prosecution rebuttal · forensic psychiatrist (retained by the Plymouth DA)

  • Reviewed her records and interviewed her twice; concluded she had a major depressive episode, not psychosis, around the time of the deaths.
  • Argued that even if she were psychotic, she likely could still distinguish right from wrong — testifying, "There's no reason that psychosis, in [and] of itself, inhibits a person's ability to know right from wrong."
  • Supports the prosecution's claim that she is criminally responsible.

The disputed account

Reported from psychiatric testimony

  • Testimony recounted Clancy's own description of the event — including that she said "Go to God" to each child — offered by experts as evidence of her delusional state, not as a confession of intent.
  • How to read that account is precisely what the two sides dispute.

These are competing expert opinions presented to the jury, not findings of fact. The jury — not any expert — decides criminal responsibility. Verbatim quotes are as reported by outlets covering the courtroom; check the sources for full context.
